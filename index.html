Pluralistic Activation Steering for Small Language Models

Blessing Ngozi Uzor1, Simona Frenda2

1African Institute for Mathematical Sciences, Cameroon   2Heriot-Watt University, Edinburgh

Code PRISM, calibration GlobalOpinionQA, evaluation

1Motivation

Language models trained mostly on feedback from Western, English-speaking users tend to answer questions about religion, family or economic values from one cultural position, as though it were the neutral one (Wang et al., 2024; AlKhamissi et al., 2024). This is not a factual error. It is a bias in whose values shaped the model, and it affects everyone who does not share that position.

The two obvious remedies do not suit settings where compute is scarce. Fine-tuning retrains the weights and must be repeated for every culture. Retrieval lengthens every prompt, which costs memory and latency and is impractical on small or offline devices. Both scale badly once there are many demographic groups and a small budget, which is exactly the setting in which this problem matters most.

Activation steering offers a third route. A model carries an internal state at every layer while it generates. If a direction in that state corresponds to a concept, adding it back during generation changes the output without retraining and without longer prompts (Turner et al., 2024). We follow Im and Li (2026), who unify four steering-vector estimators and evaluate them on Llama-2-7b-chat, and we ask whether their approach transfers to models an order of magnitude smaller.

We are not attempting persona imitation. Prompting a model to answer as an elderly religious person tends to produce stereotypes rather than perspectives, and replaces one bias with another (Jiang et al., 2023; Kwok et al., 2024). What we want instead is a single answer that genuinely holds several viewpoints at once, which is the framing used in the steerable-pluralism literature (Adams et al., 2025; Castricato et al., 2025).

Our question. Can activation steering make small language models produce answers that hold more than one cultural viewpoint, and does it work well enough to matter on models under 3B parameters? We decompose this into three parts. First, does steering toward pluralism beat a matched random perturbation, and does it beat leaving the model alone? Second, do the three demographic axes respond differently to the same steering strength, and if so, what explains the difference? Third, if the axes are not receiving comparable interventions, does correcting for that change the outcome?

We report a result we did not anticipate. The axes are not comparable at a shared multiplier, the difference is systematic and measurable, and the axis that appears unsteerable is being overwritten rather than failing to respond. We believe this affects how null results in this literature should be read.

2Method

Scope of this study is limited to inference-time activation steering on instruction-tuned models below 3B parameters. We do not evaluate fine-tuning or retrieval baselines, and all generation and judging is in English.

2.1Calibration data

We draw contrastive pairs from PRISM (Kirk et al., 2024), which records over 68,000 ratings from more than 1,500 participants across 75 countries, each tied to the demographic profile of the person who gave it. That linkage is what makes the axes possible; most preference datasets discard it.

We define a response as balanced when both poles of an axis rated a similar question highly, and one-sided when only one pole did. We match questions on exact text first and then on TF-IDF cosine similarity at 0.75 or above, which is what makes a thin axis usable at all. The difference between the pooled activations of the two sets is what we take as the pluralism direction. This yields 122 pairs on origin, 155 on religion and 166 on age.

We use three axes. Origin splits Western from non-Western by region of residence; we abandoned an earlier Global North against Global South split because that framing is economic and would have classified Mexico and Chile as non-Western despite their Hispanic-European cultural inheritance. Religion splits religious from secular by self-reported affiliation, though we note the religious group is 87% Christian. Age splits younger from older by generation rather than raw bracket, which nearly tripled the usable sample.

2.2Models and conditions

We evaluate six models spanning two families and three sizes each: SmolLM2 at 135M, 360M and 1.7B, and Qwen2.5 at 0.5B, 1.5B and 3B. We reproduce all four estimators from Im and Li (2026): mean of differences, the first principal component of the difference vectors, the first principal component of the pooled activations, and the normal of a linear classifier separating the two sets. To each we add a random control of matched magnitude, which is what separates a real effect from mere perturbation.

The steering hook adds the scaled direction to the residual stream at one layer. We apply it only to generated tokens and never to the prompt, since steering the prompt distorts how the question is read rather than shifting the answer. Within an axis we rescale the three alternative estimators to the mean-of-differences norm so that one multiplier means the same push across methods. We did not equalise across axes, and that omission turns out to be the central finding of this work.

2.3Evaluation

Our test questions are separate from the data the vectors are built on. We draw 200 questions per axis from GlobalOpinionQA (Durmus et al., 2024), which collects items from the World Values Survey and the Pew Global Attitudes survey. These are questions that already divide along the lines we steer on, and no model sees them during calibration.

We score relevance and fluency automatically, but neither measure can see pluralism. That requires a judge, so we compare every steered answer against its own unsteered baseline using two LLM judges: Mistral-Small-24B-Instruct and Prometheus-7B-v2.0. Neither is drawn from a family under test, which matters because three of our six models are Qwen and judges score their own family's outputs higher. We ruled out GPT-4 for a related reason: we did not want a Western-built judge adjudicating Western against non-Western fairness.

Judging is pairwise rather than absolute. We show every pair in both presentation orders and count a win only when the judge said so both times. Everything else resolves to a tie, which is deliberately conservative: it absorbs position bias rather than mistaking it for preference. We test each method against its own random control by resampling the difference in win rate 20,000 times at a 95% interval. Ties take 48.8% of our pairs overall and run from 5.5% to 87.5% depending on the condition, so we report every rate with its decided-pair count.

3Findings

3.1Steering beats noise more often than it beats doing nothing

Every arm, the random control included, was judged against the same unsteered baseline, so a method clears the control when its win rate over that baseline sits significantly above the control's. Twenty-five of seventy-two model-axis-method conditions clear it on Mistral and twenty-six on Prometheus. Only four and five respectively clear the unsteered baseline itself.

The gap between those two counts matters, and we think it is the most easily misread number in this work. Beating the random control means the pluralism direction moves the output more than noise of the same magnitude does. It does not mean the answer improved. Twenty-one of the significant conditions still fail to clear the unsteered baseline on either judge, and in those cases the vector is degrading the answer less than noise would rather than improving it.

The two judges agree on the size of the effect and disagree on where it falls. Both place religion at the bottom, at three conditions on Mistral and one on Prometheus out of twenty-four. They split on the other two axes, with Mistral seeing eleven on each of origin and age and Prometheus seeing sixteen and nine. We report the disagreement rather than picking the judge that suits us, and we read the shared part of it, religion's near-total absence, as the finding that the next section explains.

3.2The axes are never compared at the same strength

The multiplier does not set the steering strength on its own. It multiplies a vector whose length falls out of how far apart the balanced and one-sided activations happen to sit, so what reaches the model is the product of the two. We therefore measured the push as a fraction of the hidden state it is added to.

3.3The standard strength over-steers every axis

We re-ran the pipeline with the multiplier rescaled per axis. The rescaling used a fixed absolute reference rather than a proportional one, which lowered the delivered push on five models and raised it on one. That was not our intention, but it produces something more useful than a clean correction would have: eighteen paired comparisons in which the push moved in a known direction and the judged outcome can be checked against it.

The push fell in thirteen of the eighteen cases and the outcome improved in twelve of those. It rose in five and the outcome worsened in three. Religion is the clearest case: it sits furthest past the point at which the intervention stops nudging the answer and begins overwriting it, which is why a shared-strength comparison reads it as a dead axis. On Qwen2.5-3B, where the push fell from 48.1% to 24.7%, the religion win rate moved from 0.022 to 0.676 and the whole interval clears 0.50 on both judges. Qwen2.5-0.5B illustrates the other direction: its hidden state has a norm of only 25.5, so the fixed push was worth 77% to 82% of the representation on every axis, and it is the one model where every axis fell to zero.

Religion was never unsteerable. The same over-steering was happening to origin and age in milder form: at the standard strength every axis on every model we tested is pushed too hard. That is the finding, and it rests on eighteen paired comparisons rather than on any single model. We therefore recommend that steering strength be reported as a fraction of hidden-state norm rather than as a bare multiplier.

3.4Hyperparameters do not transfer from larger models

We used an injection depth of 45%, taken from Im and Li's (2026) result on a 7B model. A sweep across five depths shows the two families disagree. All three SmolLM2 models peak at the shallowest depth we tested, 25%, and on every one of those rows the inherited 45% falls between the two worst points available. None of the Qwen models peak there: Qwen2.5-3B peaks at 85%, Qwen2.5-0.5B at 70% and Qwen2.5-1.5B at 55%. Our main results were therefore produced at a poor injection depth for half the models tested, which means the effects we report are, if anything, understated.

Qwen2.5-1.5B deserves separate mention because it is negative at every depth. Nothing we tried made steering help that model, so its peak is the least bad option rather than a good one, and we do not read it as evidence for a depth.

4Discussion

Null results from shared-strength comparisons are unsafe. Had we stopped after Section 3.1, we would have reported that religion cannot be steered in small models, that steering is weak, and that origin is the axis that holds up. All three statements are artefacts of comparing axes at a multiplier that means different things to each of them. We think this is the most transferable thing in this work, because the design that produced it is the obvious one.

Automatic metrics are not sufficient here. On the origin axis of Qwen2.5-3B, no estimator separates from random noise of the same magnitude: the best of the four scores 0.087 against the control's 0.088. The same holds on SmolLM2-135M, where the best estimator scores 0.143 against 0.164. The judged comparison distinguishes conditions that relevance scoring reads as identical, and a study relying on relevance alone would have called both of those cells dead.

Tie rates deserve reporting. Ties take 48.8% of our judged pairs overall, 54.1% on Mistral and 43.6% on Prometheus, but the figure runs from 5.5% to 87.5% depending on the condition. A nominal sample of 200 questions therefore yields a median of 100 decided comparisons and as few as 25 in the worst case. We changed our own conclusion when we moved from 60 pairs per condition to the full 200.

Judge agreement is better than kappa suggests. Our two judges reach a pooled Cohen's κ of 0.329, falling to 0.248 averaged over the eighteen model-axis cells, which reads as only fair either way. But across 17,950 shared pairs they agree outright on 61.4% and name opposite winners on 373, or 2.08%. Almost all disagreement concerns where the tie line falls, and κ penalises a tie-against-win mismatch exactly as harshly as a full reversal.

5Conclusion

In this work we asked whether activation steering can make small language models hold more than one cultural viewpoint, and we found that the answer depends almost entirely on a quantity the method does not expose. The multiplier that appears to set steering strength does not set it; the product of that multiplier and the vector's own magnitude does, and that magnitude scales with the model's representation. Measured properly, the three demographic axes we tested occupy separate and non-overlapping bands of intervention strength, and no single setting reaches all three.

At the strength inherited from work on larger models, every axis on every model we tested is over-steered. Religion is worst affected because it starts furthest out, which is why it reads as unresponsive under a shared setting, but the effect is present on all three axes. We take from this that the method is more capable than a shared-strength evaluation makes it appear, and that the confound was doing more work than the method in our own initial results.

We do not claim to have located the operating range. Our comparisons span pushes between roughly 4% and 25% of the hidden state, all of which outperform the standard setting, but we did not sample that space finely enough to find an optimum. That is the experiment we would run next.

Limitations

Low-push improvements are partly ties. Where the delivered push fell to around 4%, tie rates roughly doubled. Some of what we score as improvement is the steered answer becoming difficult to distinguish from the baseline rather than becoming more pluralistic, and our protocol cannot separate those two. This is the objection we would raise first against our own result.

Position bias is uneven across judges. Prometheus is position-locked on 22.5% of pairs in the rescaled run that Table 2 rests on, and on 13.0% of main-run pairs, rising to 31.7% on Qwen2.5-3B. All of that is above the threshold at which we treat its verdicts as reliable. Mistral stays at 3.4% in both runs and never exceeds 5.3% for any model. Table 2 rests on Prometheus, so this is the weakest joint in the argument, though Mistral moves the same way on fifteen of the eighteen pairs.

Our judges are not independent. Prometheus 2 is fine-tuned from Mistral-7B, so both of our judges rest on the same pretraining. That the two agree is weaker evidence than agreement between unrelated judges would be, and any cultural blind spot in that base model is invisible to our protocol. We have no human validation to fall back on either, so every claim about pluralism here rests on LLM judgement alone.

Three further constraints bound what these results cover rather than threatening them: our religious group is 87% Christian, so religion here is closer to Christian-against-secular; all generation and judging is in English while the cultural axes are not; and the study is limited to inference-time steering on instruction-tuned models below 3B parameters. There is a need for culturally situated multilingual evaluation of steering that we have not attempted here.

Status

This is an ongoing project and the page will change as work continues. Every number here comes from a single complete run of all six models finished on 19 August 2026; nothing is carried over from earlier partial runs. Four things are outstanding, and we would not want the results above read as settled without them.

We have not located the operating range. Our comparisons land at roughly 4% and at roughly 25% of the hidden state, and we need a sweep between those points, measured as a fraction of the norm rather than as a multiplier, to find where the intervention actually works best. We have not added a third judge from an unrelated family, which is the only way to test whether our two judges agree because they are right or because they share a base model. We have not completed the human review of the blinded sample we exported. And we have not re-run the main experiment at the injection depth each family actually prefers, which Section 3.4 shows is not the one we used.
